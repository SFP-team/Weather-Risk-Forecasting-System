1. The model at a glance

For any location the model answers four questions from 15 winters of past weather: which production system the climate suits, when the crop flowers and is harvested, which weather risks recur, and when to plant. It describes the historical climate; it is not a forecast.

The calculation follows the season. Chill builds up in winter. Once enough chill has accumulated, warmth drives the buds to break. After budbreak, heat drives flowering, fruit development and harvest. Each stage then gets its own weather checks, such as a freeze during flowering or heavy rain during harvest.

Every winter is calculated on its own, with its own stage dates. The typical calendar shown on the dashboard is the median of the 15 winters. For Waldo it is:

In the tropics, and where the winter is too mild to trigger a calendar, the model uses a different method called the managed-cycle scan (section 7).

2. Weather data

  • Source. NASA POWER, which is based on the MERRA-2 weather reanalysis. The model uses hourly air temperature and dew point at 2 m, plus daily minimum, maximum and mean temperature, rainfall, relative humidity and solar radiation.
  • Grid cell. The weather comes from a grid cell of 0.5° latitude by 0.625° longitude, about 55 by 60 km in Florida. Every farm in the same cell gets the same weather.
  • Years. 15 winters, 2011 to 2025. The Waldo examples in this guide use the same archive.
  • Clock. Hours and days are counted in UTC (Greenwich time), which is 5 hours ahead of Florida winter time. This moves day boundaries by a few hours. The disease model instead uses the local solar day.
  • Missing data. If any hour or day inside a window is missing, that value is left blank for that winter. It is never counted as zero or as a safe year. Daily rainfall above 1,000 mm is treated as an error and left out.
  • Known bias. In a 2020 comparison with FAWN Citra and two Brazilian stations, grid minimum temperatures ran warmer than the stations and missed some sub-zero days. Chill hours and freezes are therefore probably undercounted. No correction is applied yet.

3. Winter chill

3.1 When chill is counted

Chill is counted from 1 November to 30 April in the northern hemisphere and from 1 April to 30 September in the southern hemisphere. The window is the same every year.

3.2 Chill hours

Chill hours = the number of hours in the chill window with air temperature below 7.2 °C (45 °F)

Hours below freezing also count. There is no lower limit, and warm hours do not cancel chill. For comparison the model also computes the 0 to 7.2 °C version; at Waldo in 2016–17 it gives 201 hours instead of 220.

3.3 Chill requirement and chill date

Chill requirement = 100 chill hours

Chill date = the day on which the running total of chill hours first reaches 100

The 100 hours come from Paul Adunola's later R workflow. They are the low end of the 100 to 400 hours Lyrene (2008) gives for Emerald, not a measured requirement for any one cultivar. The previous model version used 50 hours.

If a winter never reaches 100 hours, it gets no budbreak, flowering or harvest dates, and it counts as a chill-shortfall year (section 8).

3.4 Chill portions (reported, not used yet)

The Dynamic Model (Fishman, Erez and Couvillon, 1987) counts chill in portions. Cool hours build up an intermediate product, and warm hours can destroy it before it is locked in as a portion. We use the standard constants from the chillR software. The model reports chill portions beside chill hours for every winter. They do not trigger budbreak and are not used for the production-system class.

Dynamic Model equations and constants

Temperature in kelvin: TK = T + 273

xi = es / (1 + es), with s = 1.6 × 277 × (TK − 277) / TK

xs = (A0 / A1) × e(E1 − E0) / TK,   k1 = A1 × e−E1 / TK

Intermediate each hour: It = xs − (xs − S) × e−k1, where S = It−1 if It−1 < 1, otherwise S = It−1 × (1 − xi of the previous hour)

Portion added in the hour: It × xi when It ≥ 1, otherwise 0

Constants: E0 = 4153.5, E1 = 12888.8, A0 = 139,500, A1 = 2.567 × 1018

Worked example: Waldo, winter 2016–17

  • The running total reached 100 hours at 06:00 UTC on 9 January 2017 (1 a.m. in Florida). The chill date is therefore 9 January 2017.
  • The whole window collected 220 chill hours and 18.6 chill portions. 19 of the hours were at or below 0 °C.
  • 44 of the chill hours came in March, after budbreak; the March 2017 freeze fell in that month. They count toward the winter total used for the production-system class (section 6), but they cannot change the budbreak date.
  • Across all 15 winters Waldo averaged 420 chill hours (range 220 to 764) and 31.0 chill portions.

  • Is 100 hours a sensible single requirement, or should it depend on the cultivar group? Questions 1 to 3 in section 13 have space for your answer.

4. Budbreak

Once the chill requirement is met, the buds need warmth to break. The model adds up daily heat above 7 °C, starting on the chill date. Budbreak is the first day on which that sum reaches 150 degree-days.

Daily degree-days = max(daily mean temperature − 7 °C, 0)

Budbreak = the first day on which the degree-days added up from the chill date, that day included, reach 150 °C·days

  • Daily mean temperature is the grid's 24-hour average air temperature.
  • No upper temperature cap applies at this step.
  • Heat before the chill date does not count, however warm the autumn was.
  • If the sum does not reach 150 within 450 days of the start of the chill window, the winter gets no calendar.

The 150 degree-days and the 7 °C base come from Paul Adunola's R workflow. The 7 °C base was originally derived for highbush bloom (Kirk and Isaacs, 2012). Neither number has been fitted to observed budbreak dates in our data.

5. Flowering, fruit development and harvest

After budbreak the stages follow the same kind of heat sum, but the daily mean temperature is capped at 30 °C. Each sum starts on the day after its reference date.

Fruit development = the day after flowering ends to the day before harvest starts

How the translations were made. The old model used fixed days: flowering 14 to 35 days after budbreak and harvest 70 to 110 days after flowering started. For each Waldo winter we added up the degree-days inside those fixed periods and took the median across 2011 to 2025. That gave 84.9, 632.9 and 1242.4 °C·days, rounded to 85, 633 and 1242. The old rule ended flowering 35 days after budbreak, which at Waldo meant a median of 218 °C·days; the new flowering end is 375. The sums were counted from the old 50-hour chill date. Under the 100-hour requirement Waldo's median flowering start moved from 20 January to 7 February, while its median harvest moved only from 31 March to 10 May to 4 April to 12 May, because the fruit now grows in warmer weather.

These numbers describe a UF-type low-chill southern highbush. Rabbiteye and many northern highbush cultivars need more heat between flowering and harvest. If a stage is not reached within 300 days, the winter gets no calendar.

6. Production system: evergreen, semi-evergreen or deciduous

Each winter is classified on its own by two rules from Paul Adunola's R workflow. The chill used here is the total for the whole six-month window, not just the part before budbreak.

Rule A, chill only. Under 100 chill hours: evergreen. 100 to 299: semi-evergreen. 300 or more: deciduous.

Rule B, several features (used for the headline). 300 chill hours or more: deciduous.

Evergreen only if all five hold: under 100 chill hours; the coldest winter month averages a daily minimum above 7.2 °C; the coldest winter month averages a daily mean above 12.8 °C; at most 1 hour at or below 0 °C in the chill window; and no month in the chill window whose daily minimum averages 0 °C or less.

Otherwise: semi-evergreen.

"Winter months" are January to March in the north and July to September in the south. "Coldest month" means the lowest monthly average among those three, taken separately for daily minimum and daily mean temperature.

Site class = the class that at least two thirds of the winters share. If no class reaches two thirds, the dashboard says "no two-thirds majority" and shows the count for each class.

Worked example: Waldo

Winter 2016–17 had 220 chill hours, so both rules call that winter semi-evergreen: it is below 300, and it cannot be evergreen because it is above 100 and had 19 freezing hours. Its coldest month averaged a daily minimum of 10.3 °C and a daily mean of 15.2 °C.

Across 15 winters Rule B gave 11 deciduous, 4 semi-evergreen and 0 evergreen winters. 11 of 15 is 73%, above two thirds, so Waldo is classed deciduous.

Please check

  • Do the limits of 100 and 300 chill hours, 7.2 °C and 12.8 °C match how you would separate evergreen, semi-evergreen and deciduous production? Question 12 in section 13.

7. When the winter calendar applies, and the managed-cycle scan

The chill-then-heat calendar in sections 3 to 5 is used only when all three conditions hold:

  1. The site is outside the tropics, more than 23.44° from the equator.
  2. The site is not classed evergreen, and its class is known.
  3. At least 12 of the 15 winters produce a complete calendar, meaning chill is met and every stage date is reached.

Otherwise the dashboard says why, and the production window comes from the managed-cycle scan. This is how the model covers south Florida's evergreen system, Peru, Mexico and other places where growers start the crop cycle by pruning or defoliation rather than waiting for winter.

How the scan works

  1. Try 24 start dates, the 1st and 15th of every month. Each is treated as the budbreak date of a fruiting cycle, in every year from 2011 to 2025.
  2. Follow the same heat sums as section 5 from each start date to get flowering, fruit development and harvest in every year.
  3. Mark stalled cycles. If flowering lasts more than 66 days, or harvest starts more than 150 days after flowering starts, the cycle ran into cold weather. It counts as a failed cycle for flowering freeze and fruit frost. 66 days is the longest flowering period we found published (Asturias, Spain); 150 days is beyond the longest published flowering-to-harvest periods, about 116 to 120 days.
  4. Reject crop-loss starts. A start date needs at least 12 complete cycles. It is rejected if a flowering freeze (Tmin at or below −2.2 °C) or a fruit frost (Tmin at or below 0 °C) happens in half or more of its cycles.
  5. Compare the rest on five measures, where lower is better:
    • share of cycles with a flowering freeze
    • share of cycles with a fruit frost
    • share of fruit-development days with Tmax at or above 32 °C
    • share of harvest days with 10 mm of rain or more
    • share of flowering-to-harvest days at moderate or high disease risk (section 9)
  6. Keep the best starts. A start is kept when no other start is at least as good on every measure and better on at least one. Starts within one cycle (freezes) or one day per cycle (the other measures) of a kept start on every measure are kept too.
  7. Report the kept start dates, the months their median flowering and harvest touch, and the risks that recur at those starts.

The scan does not weigh one risk against another. A start can be kept because it is best on one measure, even if another measure is poor.

Limits and questions

  • The scan assumes management can start a cycle on any date. It does not model flower-bud initiation, market prices or labor.
  • In evergreen production, what sets the start of the fruiting cycle: a pruning date, defoliation or a fixed calendar date? Question 11 in section 13.

From risk years to the top risks

Risk frequency = risk years ÷ winters with complete weather for that stage window

A risk is listed only if at least 12 winters have complete data and the risk occurs in at least 50% of them

Order: highest frequency first. Risks with the same frequency share a rank. The dashboard shows up to three.

Winters with missing weather or without stage dates are left out of both counts; they are never treated as safe years. The 50% and 12-winter rules are a reporting choice, not a biological loss threshold. Where the winter calendar does not apply (section 7), the stage risks are marked not applicable. Chill shortfall is still assessed, except in the tropics and at evergreen sites. Where the scan gives the window, the dashboard lists the risks that recur at the kept start dates instead.

How the 95% range is calculated (Wilson interval)

With only 15 winters, an observed frequency is uncertain. The dashboard shows a 95% range for the long-run frequency using the Wilson score interval, with k risk years out of n winters, p = k ÷ n and z = 1.96:

center = (p + z² ÷ 2n) ÷ (1 + z² ÷ n)

half-width = z × √( p(1 − p) ÷ n + z² ÷ 4n² ) ÷ (1 + z² ÷ n)

range = center ± half-width

For Waldo's harvest heavy rain, 14 of 15 winters: p = 0.933 and the range is 70% to 99%.

Worked example: what happened at Waldo in 2016–17

  • Flowering freeze: no. The lowest flowering minimum was 4.5 °C on 10 Feb 2017.
  • Fruit frost: yes. 16 Mar 2017 had a minimum of −0.9 °C during fruit development. This is the March 2017 freeze.
  • Fruit severe heat: no. The highest fruit-development maximum was 33.6 °C on 2 Apr 2017. 4 days reached 32 °C, but none reached 35 °C.
  • Harvest heavy rain: yes. 4 Apr 2017 had 44.4 mm. The harvest window had 68.1 mm in total.
  • Disease weather: yes. On 23 Feb 2017 the Botrytis index reached 0.71 during flowering, above the 0.70 high-risk level. The anthracnose index that day was 0.33.
  • Chill shortfall: no. The winter collected 220 hours, more than 100.

9. The disease model

Disease risk uses two published infection models that UF/IFAS uses in its disease advisory systems. Both take the length of a wet period and its temperature. Both equations were fitted on strawberry. We apply them to blueberry, as the UF Blueberry Advisory System does for anthracnose.

9.1 Wet hours and wet periods

Relative humidity from hourly temperature T and dew point Td, in °C (Magnus formula):

RH = 100 × e( 17.625 Td ÷ (243.04 + Td) − 17.625 T ÷ (243.04 + T) )

Wet hour = an hour with RH of 90% or more

Wet period = a run of wet hours. Gaps of up to 3 dry hours are bridged; 4 dry hours in a row end the period.

W = number of wet hours in the period. T = average temperature of those wet hours.

In practice an hour is wet when the dew point is within about 1.5 to 2 °C of the air temperature:

Air temperature10 °C15 °C20 °C25 °C30 °C
Wet when dew point is at least8.4 °C13.4 °C18.3 °C23.2 °C28.2 °C

The 90% rule comes from Sentelhas et al. (2008), who tested it against leaf-wetness sensors at weather stations. Grid humidity is an average over a large area and has not been checked against canopy wetness.

9.2 Infection indices

Anthracnose (Wilson, Madden and Ellis, 1990):

f = −3.70 + 0.33 W − 0.069 W T + 0.0050 W T² − 0.000093 W T³

index = 1 ÷ (1 + e−f)

W is capped at 51 hours. T below 10 °C is set to 10 °C, because the curve turns upward below about 9.3 °C. The index is 0 when T is below 7 °C or above 35 °C.

Botrytis, flower infection (Bulger, Ellis and Madden, 1987):

f = −4.268 − 0.0901 W + 0.0294 W T − 0.0000235 W T³

index = 1 ÷ (1 + e−f)

W is capped at 32 hours.

Each wet period counts on the local solar day of its last wet hour (UTC plus longitude ÷ 15 hours, rounded). If several periods end on the same day, the highest index counts. A day missing any hour of data is left blank.

9.3 How days are counted

  • Flowering: days with anthracnose moderate or higher, or Botrytis moderate or higher.
  • Fruit development and harvest: days with anthracnose moderate or higher. Botrytis is not used here because its equation describes flower infection.
  • Disease-weather risk year: at least one day from flowering start to harvest end with anthracnose high, or a flowering day with Botrytis high.

11. Planting window

13. Questions for you

Q1. Chill measure

Should budbreak be triggered by chill hours below 7.2 °C (current), hours between 0 and 7.2 °C, Dynamic Model chill portions, or Utah chill units? Should hours below freezing count?

Q2. Chill requirement

What chill requirement would you use for UF low-chill southern highbush? Should the model use cultivar groups, for example very low, low and moderate chill, and with which values?

Q3. Chill window

Is 1 November to 30 April (1 April to 30 September in the south) a sensible window? Should counting start from a fixed date, from leaf drop, or from something else?

Q4. Budbreak

Is "chill first, then 150 °C·days above 7 °C" reasonable? Would you change the base temperature or the heat sum, or use a model in which warmth before the chill date also counts?

Q5. Dormancy breakers and defoliation

Should the model allow a managed budbreak date for growers who use hydrogen cyanamide, defoliation or pruning? What timing do Florida growers typically use?

Q6. Flowering

Does 85 to 375 °C·days after budbreak match flowering in your trials? How do you define flowering start and end?

Q7. Fruit development and harvest

Are 633 °C·days to first harvest and 1242 °C·days to the end of harvest right for UF southern highbush? Could you share bloom and first-ripe dates by cultivar, site and year so we can fit these numbers?

Q8. Freeze thresholds

We use −2.2 °C for flowers and 0 °C for green fruit, from one daily minimum. Would you use thresholds by stage, such as tight bud, open flower, petal fall and green fruit, or a duration below the threshold?

Q9. Heat and rain

Are 35 °C during fruit development and 10 mm of rain on a harvest day sensible thresholds? Is there a better rain measure for fruit splitting or harvest loss?

Q10. Diseases

Are anthracnose and Botrytis the right diseases to track? Should others be added, such as mummy berry or leaf rust?

Q11. Evergreen and tropical production

In evergreen production, what sets the start of the fruiting cycle: a pruning date, defoliation or a fixed calendar date? Would a pruning date be a better input than budbreak?

Q12. Production-system rules

Do 100 and 300 chill hours, 7.2 °C and 12.8 °C match how you would separate evergreen, semi-evergreen and deciduous production?

Q13. Anything else

Is any risk missing, for example spring frost after budbreak, hail, wind or bee activity? Does anything in this guide look wrong?

14. Known weak points

We compared the model with published growing calendars at 53 sites in the US, Latin America and elsewhere. Where the winter calendar applies, the middle of the modeled harvest falls inside the published harvest months at 25 of 27 southern-highbush sites. The main problems we found:

  • Peru coast. The model takes 41 to 51 days from flowering to first harvest. The Chao trial reports flowering from April and harvest from August.
  • Other blueberry types. Northern highbush blooms about two weeks late in the model in Poland and Germany. Rabbiteye harvest comes too early at Pelotas, Brazil. Where rabbiteye and northern highbush extend the season, as at Alma, Georgia and Burgaw, North Carolina, the modeled harvest ends too early.
  • Mountain farms. A farm at 2,000 m inside a warmer grid cell harvests about two months early in the model (Tafí del Valle, Argentina).
  • The 100-hour requirement in Florida. 10 of our 18 Florida, Georgia and Brazil pilot sites, all in central and south Florida, lose the winter calendar and fall back to the scan. Seven of them reach 100 chill hours in only 4 to 10 of 15 winters; the other three are evergreen.
  • Warm grid nights. Grid minimum temperatures run warm, so chill and freezes are probably undercounted.
  • Scan limits. The scan assumes budbreak can be set on any date, does not model flower-bud initiation, and does not weigh one risk against another.

15. Glossary

Chill hour
One hour with air temperature below 7.2 °C inside the chill window.
Chill portion
The Dynamic Model's unit of chill. It favors 6 to 8 °C and gives little credit below freezing or above about 13 °C.
Degree-day (°C·day)
Daily heat above the 7 °C base. A day averaging 17 °C adds 10 °C·days.
Chill date
The day the chill requirement is met.
Median
The middle value when the 15 winters are sorted. The 10% and 90% values mark the earliest and latest 10% of winters.
Risk year
A winter in which the risk event happened at least once inside its stage window.
Frequency
Risk years divided by winters with complete data.
95% range
The Wilson interval: the range of long-run frequencies consistent with the observed winters.
Grid cell
The patch of about 55 × 60 km in Florida that the weather data describe. Every farm in a cell gets the same weather.
UTC
Greenwich time, 5 hours ahead of Florida winter time.
Managed-cycle scan
The method for evergreen and tropical sites: try 24 start dates and keep those with the least adverse weather.
