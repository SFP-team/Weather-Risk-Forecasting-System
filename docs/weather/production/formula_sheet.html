How the blueberry weather model works

The formulas in plain language, for plant scientists. Live app, 15 October 2026. Temperatures in °C with °F in brackets; rain in mm.

The app takes 15 years of weather (2011 to 2025) at one location and works out, for each winter, when a low-chill southern highbush would finish its chill, break bud, bloom and be harvested. It then counts how often frost, heat, heavy rain, disease weather or poor bee weather fell in each of those stages.

  • The weather comes from NASA POWER, a record built from satellites and weather models (the MERRA-2 reanalysis). Each value describes a grid square of about 55 by 60 km in Florida, not a single field.
  • The plant is a generic low-chill southern highbush. No cultivar is named, and no setting has yet been fitted to observed bloom or harvest dates.
  • The results describe past weather. They are neither observations of plants nor a forecast.

1. Winter chillHas the bush had enough cold to end dormancy?

Blueberry flower buds need a period of cold before they can open normally. The model measures that cold in chill hours.

  • A chill hour is any hour with air temperature below 7.2 °C (45 °F). Hours below freezing count as well.
  • Counting runs from 1 November to 30 April (south of the equator, 1 April to 30 September).
  • Chill is met on the day the count reaches 50 hours. A winter that never reaches 50 gets no stage dates.
At Waldo the count passed 50 hours at 5 a.m. on 22 December 2016. That winter collected 220 chill hours in total; across 15 winters Waldo averaged 420 (range 220 to 764).

The 7.2 °C rule and the 50-hour requirement come from the supervisor's R workflow. 50 hours is a working value for low-chill southern highbush, not a measured requirement; for 'Emerald', 100 to 400 hours have been published (Lyrene 2008). The app also shows chill portions from the Dynamic Model, but they do not change any result.

2. BudbreakWhen do the buds start to grow?

Once dormancy has ended, buds grow with warmth. Warmth is measured in growing degree-days: how far each day's mean temperature is above a base temperature.

degree-days for one day = mean temperature − 7 °C

A day with a mean of 7 °C (44.6 °F) or less adds nothing. Budbreak is the first day on which the total, counted from the chill date, reaches 150 degree-days.

A day with a mean of 17 °C adds 10 degree-days; a day at 5 °C adds none. At Waldo, 22 December 2016 (mean 12.0 °C) added 5.0 and 23 December (16.0 °C) added 9.0. The total stood at 144.4 on 4 January and passed 150 on 5 January, so budbreak was 5 January 2017.

The 150 degree-days and the 7 °C base come from the supervisor's R workflow; the 7 °C base was first derived for highbush bloom (Kirk and Isaacs 2012). Neither has been fitted to observed budbreak. Degree-days here are in °C. To compare with °F degree-days (base 44.6 °F), multiply by 1.8: 150 °C-days is about 270 °F-days.

3. Bloom, fruit growth and harvestWhen does each stage happen?

degree-days for one day = mean temperature − 7 °C, with means above 30 °C counted as 30 °C

  • Bloom starts when 85 degree-days have built up after budbreak, and ends at 375.
  • Harvest starts when 633 degree-days have built up after bloom started, and ends at 1,242.
  • Fruit growth is the time between the end of bloom and the start of harvest.
  • Each stage must be reached within 300 days, or that winter has no complete calendar.
Waldo 2017: bloom 17 January to 19 February (34 days), fruit growth 20 February to 23 March, harvest 24 March to 2 May (40 days). Over 15 winters the typical Waldo season is budbreak 6 January, bloom 22 January to 24 February and harvest 29 March to 10 May.

85, 633 and 1,242 are the heat sums that matched the model's earlier fixed-day calendar at Waldo (medians for 2011 to 2025). The 290 degree-days from bloom start to bloom end match the 10% to 90% bloom span of 'Emerald' (293) and 'Jewel' (287) at Citra (Kovaleski et al. 2015). 633 to first harvest is close to Chilean on-farm sums of 593 ('O'Neal') and 665 ('Brigitta'). The 1,242 for the end of harvest has no direct support in the literature, and the 30 °C (86 °F) cap is a model assumption. Rabbiteye and most northern highbush need more heat between bloom and harvest.

The app gives the median date of the 15 winters as the typical date, with the range that covers the middle 80% of winters.

4. Production systemDoes the climate suit deciduous or evergreen production?

Each winter gets a class:

  • Deciduous: 300 chill hours or more.
  • Evergreen: fewer than 100 chill hours, and every winter month (January to March in the north, July to September in the south) has an average daily minimum above 7.2 °C (45 °F) and an average daily mean above 12.8 °C (55 °F), and there is no more than 1 hour at or below 0 °C, and no month of the chill season has an average daily minimum at or below 0 °C.
  • Semi-evergreen: everything else.

The site takes the class held by at least two-thirds of its winters (10 of 15). If no class reaches that, the site is called Transitional.

Arcadia, Florida is Evergreen in 13 of 15 winters and Semi-evergreen in 2, so it is classed Evergreen.

These rules come from the supervisor's classifier. A simpler chill-only class (under 100 hours Evergreen, 100 to 299 Semi-evergreen, 300 or more Deciduous) is shown beside it for comparison.

5. When winter sets the seasonWhich method gives the dates?

The winter-driven calendar of sections 1 to 3 is the main result only where all three of these hold:

  • the site is outside the tropics, more than 23.44° from the equator;
  • the climate is not classed Evergreen, and its class is known;
  • at least 12 of the 15 winters give a complete calendar.

Elsewhere the grower, not winter, starts the season, and the app tests start dates instead (section 9). Too little chill is counted as a risk only outside the tropics, at sites whose class is known and is not Evergreen.

7. Disease and bee weatherTwo hourly models

Infection weather

Leaf wetness is not measured, so the model estimates it.

  • An hour is wet when relative humidity is 90% or more. Humidity is calculated from air temperature and dewpoint.
  • Wet hours form a wet period, which ends once 4 or more dry hours pass. Each period has a length W (hours) and a mean temperature T (°C).
  • Anthracnose index (UF Blueberry Advisory System, Wilson et al. 1990):

    L = −3.7 + 0.33 W − 0.069 W T + 0.005 W T² − 0.000093 W T³,   index = 1 / (1 + e−L)

    W is capped at 51 hours, T below 10 °C is taken as 10 °C, and the index is 0 outside 7 to 35 °C.
  • Botrytis flower-infection index (Strawberry Advisory System, Bulger et al. 1987):

    L = −4.268 − 0.0901 W + 0.0294 W T − 0.0000235 W T³,   index = 1 / (1 + e−L)

    W is capped at 32 hours.
  • Each day takes the highest index among the wet periods that ended that day.
  • Moderate-risk day: anthracnose 0.15 or more, or Botrytis 0.50 or more during bloom. High-risk day: anthracnose 0.50 or more from bloom to the end of harvest, or Botrytis 0.70 or more during bloom. A season has disease weather if it has at least one high-risk day.

The two equations are the published advisory-system equations. Humidity here is estimated for the grid square, not the canopy, and the 90% threshold was validated with station sensors. The counts describe weather that favours infection, not disease that occurred.

Bee flight

  • A flight hour is an hour between 09:00 and 17:00 local sun time with air temperature of 12.8 °C (55 °F) or more.
  • A pollination gap is 4 or more bloom days in a row without a single flight hour.

12.8 °C is the honey bee foraging threshold (Thorp 1996). Blueberry flowers stay receptive for 3 to 5 days (UF/IFAS IN1237), and DeVetter et al. (2022) give at least 4. Rain, wind and cloud are not in the data, so flight hours are an upper bound; bumble bees fly in cooler weather.

8. How often, and when a risk is called recurring

  • For each risk, k is the number of winters with at least one qualifying day and n the number of winters with complete data. Frequency = k ÷ n.
  • A risk is listed as recurring when it happens in at least half of the winters and at least 12 winters have complete data. Recurring risks are ranked by frequency; equal frequencies share a rank.
  • Warm winter spells and dry spells are shown but never ranked, because no damage threshold is known for them.
  • The 95% range is the Wilson score interval, with p = k/n and z = 1.96:

    centre = (p + z²/2n) / (1 + z²/n),   half-width = z √(p(1 − p)/n + z²/4n²) / (1 + z²/n)

Suppose heavy harvest rain happened in 14 of 15 winters. The frequency is 93%, with a 95% range of 70% to 99%. That means the event happened in those winters; it does not mean that 93% of the fruit was lost.

The range reflects only the small number of winters, not errors in the model or the weather data. The 50% and 12-winter rules are screening conventions, not damage thresholds.

9. Evergreen and tropical sites: testing start datesWhen management, not winter, starts the season

In evergreen and tropical production the grower starts each crop, usually by pruning, so winter chill does not set the dates. There the app tests 24 possible budbreak dates, the 1st and 15th of each month, in each of the 15 years. Bloom and harvest follow the same heat rules as above.

  • A cycle stalls if bloom lasts more than 66 days, or if the first harvest comes more than 150 days after bloom starts.
  • A start date is ruled out if frost on buds, freeze in bloom, frost on young fruit or stalling happens in at least half of its cycles. It also needs at least 12 complete cycles.
  • Of the rest, a start is favourable if no other start is at least as good on every measure and better on one. The measures are those four, plus the share of fruit days with heat, of harvest days with heavy rain, of days with disease weather and of bloom days without bee flight. Starts within one affected cycle, or one affected day per cycle, of a favourable start are kept as near ties.
Arcadia, Florida: the favourable starts are 1 and 15 January, 1 and 15 February, 1 March and 15 September.

66 days is the longest published bloom season (Asturias, Spain; Campa and Ferreira 2018). 150 days is beyond the longest published bloom-to-harvest intervals, about 116 days for rabbiteye at Pelotas, Brazil, and about 120 days in Peru. The test assumes management can start a cycle on any date. It does not model flower-bud induction, the time from pruning to budbreak, or markets.

10. Planting windowWhen to plant nursery stock

Plant from 105 days to 30 days before the modelled budbreak, a 75-day window. Each winter has its own window; the typical window uses the median budbreak.

At Waldo, budbreak on 5 January 2017 gives a window of 22 September to 6 December 2016. The typical window is 23 September to 7 December.

The rule comes from the supervisor's R workflow, which labels it preliminary and gives no source. In the literature benchmark it matches 22% of published planting months at 17 sites. It needs the winter calendar of section 5, so evergreen sites get no calculated window.

11. The Climate history tab is a separate summary

Its "reference chill" counts hours from 0 to 7.2 °C between 1 November and the end of February (south of the equator, 1 May to 31 August). It leaves out freezing hours and covers a shorter season than the model's chill (section 1), so the two numbers differ and should not be compared. Cold days have a minimum below 0 °C, hot days a maximum of 35 °C or more, and the dry spell is the longest run of days with less than 1 mm of rain within a calendar year. Where hourly weather is missing, the app still shows these daily summaries but gives no crop dates, and it counts midwinter days with a maximum above 21 °C instead of warm hours.

12. Keep in mind

  • Weather is a grid estimate. Valleys, coasts and frost pockets can differ a lot from the grid square.
  • One generic low-chill southern highbush is modelled. Soil and management are not, except in the start-date test.
  • Counts of exposure are not probabilities of crop loss.
  • No setting has yet been checked against observed budbreak, bloom or harvest dates.

13. Where your expertise would help most

  • Chill requirements and heat sums for the cultivars you work with.
  • Observed budbreak, bloom and harvest dates by cultivar, site and year, so the heat sums can be fitted.
  • Damage temperatures for opening buds, open flowers and green fruit, and a heat threshold for fruit.
  • For evergreen systems, what starts the cycle and how long it takes from pruning to budbreak.
