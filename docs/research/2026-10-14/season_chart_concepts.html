Season chart: five ways to redraw it

Sketches for discussion with the breeding team, 14 October 2026. Every drawing below comes from the app's own saved results: 15 winters (2011 to 2025) from the version 3 model, with the same dates and risk counts the app shows. Choose a site to redraw all five. Concepts 1 and 2 were built into the app later on 14 October as the Season ribbon and Every winter tabs; the app version also places each published month beside the matching model window and explains sites without a chill-triggered calendar.

Site:

Why the current chart is hard to read

  1. Four kinds of thing look the same. Its seven rows hold a fixed weather window (Winter chill, the 1 Nov to 30 Apr counting window), five plant stages, a grower action (Planting) and a summary (Production window, which repeats budbreak to end of harvest). All are drawn as the same bars.
  2. Five marks, no legend on the chart. A solid bar (median start to median end), a dashed box (10th-percentile start to 90th-percentile end), black pins (median dates for chill met and budbreak), a thin hollow bar (heat building up after chill) and the long chill bar. Their meanings sit in tooltips.
  3. The bars are not real years. The solid bar joins the median start to the median end. The dashed box joins an early start to a late end, so an uncertain date looks like a long stage. Readers tend to treat values inside a bar as more likely than values outside it (Correll and Gleicher, 2014).
  4. Chill seems to last until April. The 50-hour requirement is met in late November or December, but the bar runs to 30 April, as if chill continued through bloom and harvest.
  5. The chain is hidden. Each stage starts when the previous one ends, driven by heat after chill, yet the rows look independent.
  6. Colour carries no meaning, and risks sit elsewhere. The chart has to say "colours identify stages, not severity", and the weather risks, the reason breeders look, are in separate cards.

1. Season ribbon Recommended first view

Question it answers: what is the bush doing on any date, and how much does that change between winters?

How to read it

The band is the 15 winters. On any date, its height is split by how many winters were in each stage. Where one colour fills the band, timing hardly varies; a slanted edge means that date moves between winters. Colours follow the bush: grey-blue dormant, green buds, pink flowers, green berries, blue ripe berries.

Around it

Ticks give the middle winter's date, with the range for the middle 80% of winters. Above: the model's planting rule, shaded by how many winters each day falls in the rule, and the months growers and extension guides report (hatched). Below: each weather risk sits under the stage it threatens; each square is one winter, filled when it happened.

What it fixes

One band instead of seven rows. Real winters instead of percentile boxes. Risks on the stage they hit. The model beside what growers report: at Waldo the planting rule gives September to December while Florida guides say December to February. Data: all of it is already in the app's results.

2. Every winter Evidence view

Question it answers: what actually happened in each winter?

Order rows by

How to read it

One row per winter, like a field notebook. A mark sits on the stage where the weather event happened that winter. On the right, that winter's total chill. Ordering by budbreak lines up the warm and cold winters.

What it fixes

There is nothing statistical to decode. Odd years stand out, and a breeder can ask what happened in a given winter and check it against their own notes.

Limit

A mark says an event happened during that stage, not on which day. Exact frost and rain dates need one more field from the API.

3. Two clocks How the dates are calculated

Question it answers: why do the stages fall on these dates?

How to read it

Left: each winter's total chill against the model's requirement. Right: once the requirement is met, the bush counts heat (degree-days above 7 °C). Each line is one winter; a stage starts where its line crosses that stage's threshold. A steep line is a warm spring.

What it fixes

It shows the mechanism, so the dates stop looking arbitrary. The thresholds carry their numbers from the breeder guide (P6, P10, P12 to P15), so a breeder can see what changing one would do.

Limit

Lines are drawn straight between the model's own threshold dates. The real daily curves need the API to return the daily heat sum.

4. Season wheel For comparing sites

Question it answers: what does this site's year look like, and how does it compare with another site?

How to read it

The year as a clock with midwinter at the top, so northern and southern sites line up. Middle ring: model stages, solid for the median dates and pale for the middle 80% of winters. Outer hatched ring: what growers report. Inner rings: planting, model rule and guides. Red dots: weather risks, larger when more frequent.

Where it fits

Site fingerprints side by side, for example on the benchmark page or when choosing trial sites. It loops naturally across the new year.

Limit

Not for reading exact dates: people judge angles and arcs less accurately than positions on a line (Cleveland and McGill, 1984).

5. Same chart, fewer puzzles Smallest change

If the team wants the current layout, these fixes remove most of the questions.

Changes

A legend on the chart. Rows grouped as weather, plant and grower. Chill drawn solid only until the requirement is met. Stages joined in a chain. The production window becomes a bracket instead of a row. Published months hatched behind the model.

Wording

"Analysis window" becomes "typical winter". Stage names describe the bush ("Berries growing"), and every range says what it covers ("middle 80% of winters").

Data

All available now; a front-end change only.

Suggested order

  1. Make the season ribbon (1) the default chart, with the season sentence above it.
  2. Put "Every winter" (2) behind a switch, and the two clocks (3) behind "How the dates are calculated".
  3. Use the wheel (4) only for comparing sites.
  4. If the team wants a change this week, apply the fixes in (5) first; they need no new data.

Questions for the scientists: Does the ribbon match how you think about the season? Should published calendars sit on the main chart? Are the bush colours and icons clear? After seeing the two clocks, which threshold would you change first?

Evidence behind the design choices

  • Correll, M. and Gleicher, M. (2014). Error bars considered harmful: exploring alternate encodings for mean and error. IEEE Transactions on Visualization and Computer Graphics 20(12). Readers judge values inside a bar as more likely; gradient and violin displays reduce this.
  • Kay, M., Kola, T., Hullman, J. and Munson, S. (2016). When (ish) is my bus? User-centered visualizations of uncertainty in everyday, mobile predictive systems. CHI 2016. Fernandes, M. et al. (2018). Uncertainty displays using quantile dotplots or CDFs improve transit decision-making. CHI 2018. One dot per outcome helps people judge uncertainty and decide.
  • Hullman, J., Resnick, P. and Adar, E. (2015). Hypothetical outcome plots outperform error bars and violin plots for inferences about reliability of variable ordering. PLOS ONE 10(11).
  • Gigerenzer, G. and Hoffrage, U. (1995). How to improve Bayesian reasoning without instruction: frequency formats. Psychological Review 102(4). Counts such as "4 of 15 winters" are understood better than percentages.
  • Cleveland, W. S. and McGill, R. (1984). Graphical perception. Journal of the American Statistical Association 79(387). Position on a common scale is read most accurately; angle and area less so.
  • Tufte, E. R. (1990). Envisioning Information. Small multiples: the same small chart repeated, one per case.
