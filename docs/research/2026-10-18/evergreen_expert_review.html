1. Summary

For any place on land, the tool takes 15 years of weather, 2011 to 2025, and estimates when a generic low-chill southern highbush would bloom and be harvested. It then counts how often frost, heat, heavy rain, disease weather or poor bee weather hit each stage.

  • Deciduous and semi-evergreen sites. Winter chill starts the calendar. Our literature benchmark has 53 sites, 46 of them growing southern highbush, and 30 of those get this calendar. The middle of the modelled harvest falls inside the published harvest months at 26 of the 30. The middle of modelled bloom falls inside published bloom months at 14 of the 20 sites that publish them.
  • Evergreen and tropical sites. Winter does not start a calendar there. The tool instead tests 24 budbreak dates and keeps those with the least damaging weather. It ignores pruning, flower-bud formation and markets, and it ripens fruit too fast in warm places. It cannot yet tell an evergreen grower when to prune. We think this is the weakest part of the tool.
  • Our proposal. An evergreen planner that starts from the pruning date. It checks flower-bud formation against day length and temperature, ripens fruit with a recalibrated temperature response, scores weather risk, and finds the pruning dates that hit a chosen market window in most years.
  • What we need from you. Whether this matches how evergreen growers decide, better starting values, and any records of pruning, bloom and harvest dates we could calibrate against.

2. How the tool calculates a season today

2.1 Weather data

The weather comes from NASA POWER, which is built on the MERRA-2 reanalysis. Its grid is 0.5° by 0.625°, about 55 by 60 km in Florida and 55 by 70 km near the equator. The tool uses hourly air temperature and dewpoint, and daily minimum, maximum and mean temperature, rain, humidity, wind and solar radiation for 2011 to 2025.

Every result describes open-field weather for a whole grid square. Tunnels, pots, valleys, coasts and frost pockets are not modelled. In earlier pilot checks the grid's minimum temperatures ran warmer than station records, so the tool probably undercounts chill and freezes.

2.2 Production-system class

  • Deciduous. 300 chill hours or more in the winter.
  • Evergreen. Fewer than 100 chill hours. Every winter month, January to March in the north or July to September in the south, has an average daily minimum above 7.2 °C (45 °F) and an average daily mean above 12.8 °C (55 °F). No more than 1 hour reaches 0 °C (32 °F) or lower, and no month of the chill season has an average minimum at or below 0 °C.
  • Semi-evergreen. Everything else.

A site takes the class held by at least two-thirds of its winters, 10 of 15. If no class reaches that, the site is Transitional.

2.3 Deciduous and semi-evergreen sites: the winter calendar

  • Chill. Every hour below 7.2 °C (45 °F) counts, including hours below freezing, from 1 November to 30 April. South of the equator the count runs from 1 April to 30 September. Chill is met at 50 hours.
  • Budbreak. From the chill date, each day adds its mean temperature minus 7 °C (44.6 °F), or nothing on colder days. Budbreak is the day the total reaches 150 degree-days.
  • Bloom and harvest. Bloom runs from 85 to 375 degree-days after budbreak. Harvest runs from 633 to 1,242 degree-days after bloom starts. For these stages a daily mean above 30 °C (86 °F) counts as 30 °C.
  • Where it applies. Sites more than 23.44° from the equator, whose class is known and is not Evergreen, and where at least 12 of 15 winters give a complete calendar. Everywhere else the start-date scan of section 2.5 runs.

Example

Waldo, Florida, winter 2016 to 2017. Chill was met on 22 December, budbreak came on 5 January, bloom ran from 17 January to 19 February and harvest from 24 March to 2 May. Over 15 winters the typical Waldo season is budbreak on 6 January, bloom from 22 January to 24 February and harvest from 29 March to 10 May.

Where the numbers come from. The 7.2 °C rule, the 50-hour requirement and the 150 degree-days come from our lab's earlier R workflow. None has been fitted to observed budbreak, and for 'Emerald' 100 to 400 chill hours have been published (Lyrene 2008). 85, 633 and 1,242 are the Waldo heat sums that matched the tool's earlier fixed-day calendar. The 290 degree-days from bloom start to bloom end match the 10% to 90% bloom span of 'Emerald' (293) and 'Jewel' (287) at Citra (Kovaleski et al. 2015). The planting window, 105 to 30 days before modelled budbreak, matches only 22% of published planting months at 17 benchmark sites.

The tool lists a risk as recurring when it happens in at least half of the winters with complete data, and only if at least 12 winters have complete data. A count of exposure is not a probability of crop loss.

2.5 Evergreen and tropical sites today: the start-date scan

  • The tool forces budbreak on the 1st and 15th of every month, 24 start dates, in each of the 15 years. Bloom and harvest follow the heat rules of section 2.3.
  • A cycle stalls if bloom lasts more than 66 days or first harvest comes more than 150 days after bloom starts.
  • A start date is ruled out if frost on buds, freeze in bloom, frost on young fruit or stalling happens in at least half of its cycles.
  • The remaining start dates are compared on those four, plus heat on fruit, rain at harvest, disease weather and bloom days without bee flight. The best trade-offs are called favourable.
  • The scan assumes management can start a cycle on any date. It does not model pruning, the delay from pruning to budbreak, flower-bud formation or markets.

Examples

Arcadia, Florida, Evergreen in 13 of 15 winters. The favourable starts are 1 and 15 January, 1 and 15 February, 1 March and 15 September. Growers report bloom in December to February and harvest in March to May.

Chao, Peru, Evergreen in 15 of 15 winters. The favourable starts run from 15 June to 1 September. A 15 June start gives bloom from 22 June to 15 July and harvest from 11 August to 29 September. In a published Chao trial, growers pruned in early March, bloom started in April, and harvest ran from 1 August to 15 January.

A test version of the app, not yet public, adds a year-round planner with per-year cycles for a chosen start date, weekly weather hazards, a market-window search and El Niño months. We can show it during the meeting.

What we see

  • Florida evergreen sites. The scan does not narrow harvest to the March to May window growers use. At Homestead it calls every month possible. Lyrene (1996) reports that south of 28°N in Florida, low-chill plants can flower and fruit on new growth for 4 to 8 months, so part of that spread may be real. Growers still aim for March to May.
  • Peru. Harvest months come out close, but modelled bloom at Chao is two months late, because the model ripens fruit far faster than growers see. The grid's April to August means at Chao are 19 to 21 °C (66 to 70 °F). At those temperatures 633 degree-days take about 46 days, while growers see about 4 months from April bloom to August harvest. At Ica the weather does not rule out a single start date.
  • Highlands. Growers at Guasca pick all year. The model picks only from December to March.
  • Management and cover. Wolseley, Moguer and Tzaneen are run as evergreen or mixed systems, partly under tunnels. Open-field climate classes them deciduous or semi-evergreen, so the winter calendar runs there and misses.
  • El Niño 2023. In our grid, monthly means from April to October 2023 ran up to 2.1 °C (3.8 °F) above the 2011 to 2025 average at Olmos, up to 1.8 °C (3.2 °F) at Chao and up to 1.1 °C (2.0 °F) at Ica. Reports describe a fall and winter 4 to 5 °C (7 to 9 °F) warmer than normal in the north, with failed flowering, while Ica held steady. The grid shows the same north-south pattern, only smaller. It averages a whole grid square, and monthly means hide night minima.

5. Proposed evergreen planner

This is a plan. We have not built it, and we would change it after this review.

5.1 Two kinds of evergreen site, read from the weather

For every day of every year, the planner would mark whether flower buds can form there: day length short enough and temperature low enough. That gives each site an induction calendar. We think the calendar's shape matters more than any single setting, because it changes what advice makes sense.

  • Seasonal sites. Buds can form in only one season, as in Florida. Pruning builds wood for that season. The advice is the latest post-harvest hedging date that still leaves mature shoots when the season starts, plus the natural bloom and harvest windows.
  • Programmable sites. Buds can form in most months, as on the Peruvian coast. The pruning date sets the harvest date. The advice is which pruning dates hit a chosen market window in most years.

5.2 The crop cycle, started from pruning

Pruning comes first, then budbreak about 15 days later. Shoots grow and stop. Flower buds form once enough inductive days have passed after growth stops. The buds develop and open, and fruit ripens at a rate set by a fitted temperature response. The planner would carry two cultivar classes. One needs short days and cool temperatures, like 'Arcadia'. The other is day-neutral or everbearing, like the UF selection FL16-64.

5.4 What a grower would see

  • The site type, with the weeks when flower buds can form in most years.
  • At programmable sites, for a market window the grower picks, the range of pruning dates whose first pick lands in the window in at least 10 of 15 years, with the typical bloom and first-pick dates.
  • At seasonal sites, the latest hedging date that still leaves time for flower buds, and the natural bloom and harvest windows.
  • For each pruning date, the years with poor flower-bud formation, such as the warm El Niño winter of 2023, and how often freeze or heat hit bloom, rain hit harvest and heat followed pruning.

5.5 How we would test it

  1. Predict bloom and first harvest for the published pruning dates in section 4, leaving each site out of the fit in turn. Most predictions should land within 3 weeks.
  2. Across the 16 sites where the scan runs today, match or beat today's 78% and 76% harvest-month agreement, and put Chao's bloom in April and May.
  3. Flag poor flower-bud formation in 2023 at Olmos and Chao, but not at Ica.
  4. Give bloom in December to February and harvest in March to May at Arcadia and Sebring.
  5. Leave every deciduous result unchanged.

Your top three changes, most important first.

7. Data that would help

For any evergreen field, by cultivar, place, year and system (open field, tunnel or pots):

  • pruning or hedging date and how hard it was, plus any leaf stripping, hydrogen cyanamide or fertilizer pause;
  • dates of budbreak, 10% and 50% bloom, first pick, 50% harvest and last pick;
  • freeze, heat or storm damage, with dates.

Spreadsheets in any format are fine. We keep grower and breeder records private and do not publish them with the project code.
